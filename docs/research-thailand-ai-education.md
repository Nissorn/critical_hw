# Thailand AI-in-education evidence note

**Scope.** This note stress-tests a concept for teacher-approved AI-generated analytical-thinking homework, AI-assisted formative feedback, adaptive support groups, and teacher-visible academic-integrity signals. Evidence is Thailand-specific where possible. “Hypothesis” means a product question to test, not a recommendation or decision. Percentages are reported as published; they are not estimates of all Thai children unless the source says so.

## Findings and limitations by decision area

### 1. Access is heterogeneous, not binary

The strongest Thailand-specific access evidence is the UN mapping, which combines NSO, MOE/OBEC, MDES, NBTC and PISA data. It explicitly warns that the analysis was limited to available secondary data, excluded non-formal education, and covered school ICT mainly under OBEC. The findings therefore support designing for low bandwidth, shared devices and uneven school infrastructure; they do **not** justify assuming every learner has a private laptop, quiet study space, or stable broadband. Sources: *E-learning in Thailand: Mapping the digital divide* — ITU/UNESCO/UNICEF — 17 Aug 2021 — [UN Thailand publication](https://thailand.un.org/en/140320-e-learning-thailand-mapping-digital-divide); [PDF, pp. 13 and 25–31](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2021-08/Presentation%20on%20mapping%20of%20digital%20divide.pdf).

The mapping also reports a potential affordability problem: more than three quarters of connected households used mobile broadband, and its illustrative estimate for 20 hours/week of live online group learning on a prepaid mobile package was **THB 4,629/month per student**. This is an example calculation based on 2020 prices/assumptions, not a current market price or a claim about what families actually spend. Sources: *E-learning in Thailand: Mapping the digital divide* — ITU/UNESCO/UNICEF — 17 Aug 2021 — [PDF, pp. 8 and 31](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2021-08/Presentation%20on%20mapping%20of%20digital%20divide.pdf).

### 2. Outcomes justify testing reasoning, but not promising score gains

PISA is useful because its mathematics framework embeds solving problems, and its creative-thinking module directly includes social and scientific problem-solving tasks. Thailand’s low baseline shares and socioeconomic gap are signals to measure whether a product improves reasoning processes and access for disadvantaged learners. They are not evidence that an AI-generated assignment, grouping algorithm or automated feedback will cause improvement. PISA’s Thailand country note says all data met PISA quality standards, while the creative-thinking factsheet reports a specific 2022 sample/assessment; neither is a randomized intervention study. Sources: *PISA 2022 Country Notes: Thailand* — OECD — 4 Dec 2023 — [country note](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i-and-ii-country-notes_ed6fbcc5-en/thailand_6138f4af-en.html); *PISA 2022 Creative Thinking Factsheet: Thailand* — OECD — 17 Jun 2024 — [factsheet](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-results-2022-volume-iii-factsheets_041a90f1-en/thailand_ff214311-en.html).

### 3. Teacher review is a scarce resource

The EEF/EEFI workload report is especially relevant to teacher-approved generation and formative feedback: a teacher may value better personalization while lacking time to inspect every generated item or AI comment. The report is a recent official EEF communication, but the page does not disclose the underlying study’s sampling frame, questionnaire, response rate or full methods. Treat the figures as a warning signal and pilot input, not a national workload census. PISA adds a different, student-reported signal: many students perceive teacher interest/help, but a meaningful minority report device distraction. Sources: *The Small-School Teacher Crisis* — EEF — 13 Jul 2026 — [EEF article](https://en.eef.or.th/2026/07/13/the-small-school-teacher-crisis/); *PISA 2022 Country Notes: Thailand* — OECD — 4 Dec 2023 — [country note](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i-and-ii-country-notes_ed6fbcc5-en/thailand_6138f4af-en.html).

### 4. Policy direction is “use with governance,” not evidence of universal readiness

The MOE handbook (2025) and the Permanent Secretary office’s 2025 AI-for-Education event show that AI is now an explicit Thai education policy topic, with emphasis on ethics, human oversight and protecting learners. They do not show that schools have common procurement, assessment or acceptable-use rules. ChulaGENIE is a useful higher-education precedent for private, access-controlled deployment, but its launch announcement is not an independent security audit or an impact study. Sources: MOE AI handbook — Ministry of Education/OBEC — 31 Mar 2025 — [official page](https://www.moe.go.th/e-book/%E0%B8%84%E0%B8%B9%E0%B9%88%E0%B8%A1%E0%B8%B7%E0%B8%ad%E0%B8%81%E0%B8%B2%E0%B8%A3%E0%B9%83%E0%B8%8A%E0%B9%89-ai-%E0%B8%aa%E0%B8%9e%E0%B8%90/); AI for Education event — Office of the Permanent Secretary, MOE — 4 Sep 2025 — [official report](https://ops.moe.go.th/ai-for-education/); ChulaGENIE — Chulalongkorn University — 29 Nov 2024 — [announcement](https://www.chula.ac.th/en/highlight/203964/).

### 5. Academic integrity evidence is primarily guidance, not Thai prevalence data

I found authoritative guidance on GenAI risks, but not a reliable national Thai K–12 dataset quantifying plagiarism or GenAI-assisted cheating. UNESCO’s guidance is global; the MOE’s AI handbook is Thai policy guidance but its indexed page does not publish a prevalence study. Therefore, do not state that “Thai students are cheating with AI at rate X.” Instead, test whether students can explain, source and revise their work, and let each school define permitted use. Process evidence can also reduce the false-positive/false-negative risk of treating an AI detector as a verdict. Sources: *Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research* — UNESCO — Sept 2023 — [document](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000386693); MOE AI handbook — Ministry of Education/OBEC — 31 Mar 2025 — [official page](https://www.moe.go.th/e-book/%E0%B8%84%E0%B8%B9%E0%B9%88%E0%B8%A1%E0%B8%B7%E0%B8%AD%E0%B8%81%E0%B8%B2%E0%B8%A3%E0%B9%83%E0%B8%8A%E0%B9%89-ai-%E0%B8%aa%E0%B8%9e%E0%B8%90/).
### 6. Thai university guardrails are concrete but institution-specific

Two Thai university policies make the integrity implications operational. Chulalongkorn’s 17 Jul 2023 guidance asks instructors to define permitted AI scope in the syllabus, redesign assessment away from work AI can directly produce, require citation/disclosure, and prohibit uploading confidential or personal information without consent. KKU AI Sphere’s 9 Dec 2025 teaching guidance similarly says instructors should verify AI outputs and design for analytical thinking; it says students may not submit all or most AI-generated work unless the instructor permits it, and must cite/disclose AI use. Both are normative university guidance, not national K–12 rules, prevalence estimates, or effectiveness evaluations. Sources: *Chulalongkorn University Principles and Guidelines for using AI Tools* — Chulalongkorn University, Office of Academic Affairs — 17 Jul 2023 — [official policy](https://www.chula.ac.th/en/news/125190/); *แนวทางการใช้ปัญญาประดิษฐ์ด้านการเรียนการสอน* — KKU AI Sphere / Khon Kaen University — 9 Dec 2025 — [official guidance](https://ai.kku.ac.th/%e0%b9%81%e0%b8%99%e0%b8%a7%e0%b8%97%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%87%e0%b8%81%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%a3%e0%b9%83%e0%b8%8a%e0%b9%89%e0%b8%9b%e0%b8%b1%e0%b8%8d%e0%b8%8d%e0%b8%b2%e0%b8%9b%e0%b8%a3%e0%b8%b0%e0%b8%94%e0%b8%b4/).


## Product implications as hypotheses to test

1. **Low-bandwidth hypothesis:** If core homework, feedback and teacher review work on a phone, tolerate intermittent connectivity, and support downloadable/printable handoffs, then participation gaps will be smaller than with live, synchronous, computer-first workflows. Test by rural/urban, device type, and offline completion rates; do not assume the 2021 access profile is current.
2. **Shared-device hypothesis:** If a learner can complete meaningful work in short sessions with autosave and clear account/session separation, then households sharing devices will have fewer missed submissions than workflows requiring a continuous private laptop session.
3. **Teacher-capacity hypothesis:** If AI generation is bounded by teacher-approved templates/rubrics and presents only reviewable exceptions, then teachers in small schools will spend less review time without surrendering approval. Measure minutes per assignment, correction rate and teacher-reported workload; EEF’s workload figures are a warning signal, not a guaranteed baseline.
4. **Process-over-product hypothesis:** If assignments collect drafts, source checks, reflection and a short explanation/defense, then schools can support legitimate AI use while making substitution for learning more visible than a final-answer-only submission. Test agreement between process evidence and teacher judgments; do not treat detector scores as proof.
5. **Equitable grouping hypothesis:** If adaptive support groups use transparent, teacher-overridable evidence and are audited by socioeconomic/location/device-access strata, then support may improve without turning historical disadvantage into a permanent label. Test subgroup outcomes and reassignment rates; PISA’s 61-point mathematics SES gap is context, not a causal model.
6. **Thai governance hypothesis:** If the product exposes data retention, access controls, age/consent flows, AI-use declarations and an educator escalation path in Thai, then adoption readiness will be higher under the MOE’s 2025 ethics/oversight direction than with an opaque consumer chatbot integration. Test with schools; policy pages do not prove procurement requirements.
7. **Attention hypothesis:** If device use is bounded by short, purposeful activities and teachers can choose paper/oral alternatives, then digital distraction will be less likely to undermine classroom work. Test against baseline distraction and completion measures; PISA reports associations/self-reports, not causality.
8. **Outcome-measurement hypothesis:** If pilots use reasoning-specific rubrics and pre/post tasks aligned to local curriculum—not only completion, grades or model confidence—then the team can detect whether analytical thinking improves rather than merely whether generated work looks polished. PISA creative-thinking and problem-solving indicators motivate this measurement choice but cannot validate the product by themselves.
